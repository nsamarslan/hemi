# UA-39 — Neuro-Anatomy of the Out-of-Body Experience: Can the Self-Model Be Fooled?

This dossier examines how the feeling of "being outside the body" is produced in the laboratory, and what that production does and does not say about the experience's ontological status. Temporoparietal junction stimulation, virtual-reality self-location drift and clinical seizure observations show that self-location is a **computed model**. But the dossier does not stop there: it shows that the only honest way to test "verifiable perception" claims is **hidden-target** studies — and marks the point where the question remains experimentally open.

## 1. Intersection Map: Neurology, Virtual Reality and Death Research Look at the Same Phenomenon

Four lines intersect here:

1. **Clinical neurology** — out-of-body experiences and autoscopy in epilepsy patients.
2. **Cognitive neuroscience** — self-location, body ownership, and spatial self-models.
3. **Virtual reality and experimental psychology** — the full-body illusion, the rubber hand, body swapping.
4. **Near-death experience research** — prospective cohorts and hidden-target studies.

Why do they not merge? Because merging them produces a sentence that satisfies neither side: **the feeling of "being outside the body" is a fully producible self-model event; but that does not mean the producible is the real — and claimed verifiable perception is a separate experimental question.**

## 2. Anomaly Inventory (with citations)

### 2.1 Clinical observation: out-of-body experiences by stimulation

Blanke et al. (2002, *Nature* 419:269–270) showed that electrical stimulation around the right temporoparietal junction (TPJ) triggered an **out-of-body experience** (seeing oneself from above) in an epilepsy patient. In larger series (Blanke & Mohr, 2005, *Brain* 128:1312–1322), lesions around the TPJ were associated with cases of autoscopy, heautoscopy and the "feeling of presence". Arzy et al. (2006, *Nature* 443:287) induced an **illusory shadow person** by stimulating the left TPJ: the patient perceived a presence standing behind her.

The shared meaning: **information about "where the self is" is the output of a computation** — and that computation can be broken.

### 2.2 The full-body illusion: bringing self-location into the laboratory

Lenggenhager, Tadi, Metzinger and Blanke (2007, *Science* 317:1096–1099) produced **illusory self-seeing** in participants filmed from behind: tactile stimulation made participants feel their own body at the "virtual" body in front of them. Ehrsson (2007, *Science* 317:1048) showed in a similar setup that self-location genuinely shifts; Petkova and Ehrsson (2008, *PLoS ONE* 3:e3832) and Slater et al. (2010, *PLoS ONE* 5:e10564) replicated **body swapping** and virtual-body ownership in VR.

This shows that the question "where is my body, and whose is it?" is derived from the **agreement** of visual, tactile and vestibular signals. When agreement breaks, self-location drifts.

### 2.3 Mechanism: the multisensory self-model

The framework of Blanke and Metzinger (Blanke & Metzinger, 2009, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 13:7–13) describes bodily self-consciousness as a **multisensory model**: visual, tactile, proprioceptive and vestibular signals contribute with different weights to the components of the "body schema" (self-location, self-ownership, self-perspective). Whichever signal system becomes dominant, the structure of the experience changes accordingly.

Lopez and Blanke (2011, *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* 35:1986–2001) documented a special role for the vestibular system in self-location: out-of-body experiences and autoscopic illusions appear **more often** in vestibular disorders. This suggests that when the self-model loses its "gravity sensor", it easily transports itself upward.

### 2.4 The hardest result from VR research

VR studies (e.g. Maselli & Slater, 2013, *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 7:83; the review by Bourdin et al., 2017) showed self-location can be shifted **within minutes** and that this is reflected in physiological measures. The sense of "I am outside my body" is thus a phenomenon producible by sensory conflict alone, without any special spiritual state.

### 2.5 But: the "verifiable perception" claim

So far the evidence says "the self-location model can drift". But some near-death and spontaneous out-of-body experiences include **verifiable perception** claims: the patient correctly describing objects they would have to see from above their own body, surgical instruments, conversations, hidden visual targets.

Experimentally, such claims can be tested with **hidden-target methodology** — and have been:

- van Lommel et al. (2001, *The Lancet* 358:2039–2045): a prospective cohort of 62 NDE cases; features reported that psychological models of the time struggled to explain, but **verifiable-perception testing** (hidden targets) was not systematic.
- Parnia et al. (2014, *Resuscitation* 85:1799–1805, AWARE): 2060 cardiac arrests, 140 interviews; 9 patients reported memories, only 2 with auditory recall; the hidden-target images were **verified in no case**.
- Parnia et al. (2023, *Resuscitation* 188:109839, AWARE-II): 567 patients, 53 with signs of consciousness, and 1 patient with a **verified** sequence of memories (tablet screen, automated external voice, in a window consistent with EEG recording) — the strongest single verified case in the literature, but at the level of **one case**.

**An honest assessment:** hidden-target methodology is the only honest test of the claim, and the present evidence has **not** provided mass verification; at the same time, there is one striking single-case result. The dossier stays **open** at this point.

### 2.6 A methodological caution: illusion ≠ all cases

The full-body illusion and spontaneous out-of-body experiences are not the same thing: the illusion requires an **external visual cue** under laboratory conditions; spontaneous experiences typically arise without visual cues, even with closed eyes, and the "view from above" perspective remains stable. "One mechanism, one explanation" is therefore an **oversimplification** (see Blanke & Metzinger 2009's own limits).

### 2.7 Meditation and voluntary transition

Advanced practitioners (particularly those using advanced Gateway/OBE protocols) report **voluntarily** shifting self-location. This has not been systematically documented in the laboratory; but VR and neuroimaging studies suggest self-location is a trainable model.

### 2.8 Ontological accounting: three possible interpretations

1. **Model drift:** all OBEs are decouplings of the multisensory model (reductionist reading).
2. **Decoupling:** in some cases the model decouples and **another information source** enters (dualist reading).
3. **Mixed:** most OBEs are model drift; in a small number of verifiable cases, an additional information channel is involved (mixed reading).

The dossier's stance: the evidence makes **1 certain**, **2 possible but unproven**, and positions **3 as the most fruitful testable hypothesis**. And the test's name is hidden target.

## 3. Three-Level Excavation

### Level 1 — Observation
- TPJ stimulation produces out-of-body experience and the shadow-person illusion.
- The full-body illusion shifts self-location via VR and camera setups.
- OBE and autoscopy are more frequent in vestibular disorders.
- Hidden-target studies (AWARE, AWARE-II) have produced no mass verification to date; there is one strong case.
- Spontaneous OBEs carry features unlike the illusion conditions.

### Level 2 — Pattern
**(A) Self-location is a computation.** A weighted average of visual, tactile, vestibular and proprioceptive signals produces the answer to "where am I". When the computation's inputs conflict, the answer drifts.

**(B) Threshold behaviour.** Drift does not occur under small conflicts; it occurs when the conflict crosses a **threshold** (in VR, when visual-tactile delay exceeds a certain window, the illusion either collapses or is never established). This is MP-3 (critical point) reflected in the spatial self-domain.

**(C) Channel separation.** Model drift and "verifiable perception" are **two separate claims**; demonstrating one neither proves nor refutes the other. Any discussion that does not preserve this distinction falls into either naive dualism or naive reductionism.

### Level 3 — Why is it designed this way?
Because self-location **cannot have a single source.** The body moves continuously in space; the visual scene offers no fixed reference frame. A robust self-location requires the system to combine **multiple references** — and to weight others when one reference is temporarily unreliable (darkness, water, rotation, weightlessness, the hypnagogic state).

The side effect of this design is inevitable: **when inputs conflict, the system can take a non-existent location to be "correct".** Seeing oneself from outside is therefore not a malfunction of the architecture but its **inevitable by-product**.

## 4. HIDDEN PATTERN CANDIDATE: HZ-39 — The TPJ Self-Model Gateway

> **Candidate Law (HZ-39).** Self-location is a multisensory estimate of visual, tactile, vestibular and proprioceptive signals; when the conflict crosses a threshold, the estimate re-locates (the out-of-body view). This mechanism is **demonstrated in the laboratory**. However, the "verifiable perception" claim reported in spontaneous cases must be tested **independently** of this mechanism through hidden-target studies.

Simplified expression:

**Self_location ≈ argmax_g [ Π_g × L_g(visual, tactile, vestibular, proprioceptive) ]** &nbsp;&nbsp; and if |Conflict| > E_threshold, location drifts.

**Three predictions:**
1. **Threshold prediction:** as the tactile-visual synchrony delay in VR increases, self-location drift should occur through an **abrupt** (threshold-like) transition, not gradually.
2. **Vestibular weight:** when the vestibular signal is degraded (galvanic stimulation), the visual-tactile conflict threshold required for drift should **fall**.
3. **Channel separation:** under model-drift conditions (VR), participants' hidden-target accuracy should remain at **chance**; otherwise the model claim weakens.

**Discriminating test (HZ-39/1 — threshold transition).** In a VR self-location setup, the synchrony delay is scanned parametrically (e.g. 0–500 ms); at each condition the magnitude of drift and subjective intensity are measured. Prediction: a threshold-like profile (a sharp jump), not a continuous increase.

**Second test (HZ-39/2 — vestibular modulation).** In the same setup, galvanic vestibular stimulation on/off; prediction: with stimulation on, the drift threshold **falls** (the vestibular reference weakens).

**Third test (HZ-39/3 — hidden target, the decisive experiment).** In clinical settings with high near-death probability (cardiac ICU, resuscitation), hidden visual/auditory targets are embedded and survivors' recall is tested with blinded assessment (a scaled-up AWARE-II protocol). The prediction statement is explicit: if the claim is true, the verification rate should rise **above** chance; the protocol must be designed so that it can both confirm and refute. This is the **single most important experiment** in this area.

## 5. Evidence Thermometer: 🟢 (self-model decoupling) / 🟡 (verifiable-perception claims)

**Why 🟢?** That self-location is a multisensory model, the role of the TPJ, VR illusions and the vestibular connection were shown repeatedly in independent laboratories. "Self-location is computed" is no longer controversial.

**Why 🟡?** The "verifiable perception" claim is independent of this mechanism, and the current evidence base is mixed: no verification in AWARE, one strong case in AWARE-II. Also, the full-body illusion does not reproduce all features of spontaneous OBEs.

> **If refuted, what survives?** Even if the hidden-target program returns entirely empty, this dossier stands: the computed nature of self-location is demonstrated and is valuable for neuroscience, VR therapy and the treatment of dissociative disorders. The opposite outcome (verification) would make this dossier the **highest-impact** result in the whole project. Both directions produce information; the dossier therefore cannot lose.

## 6. Practical Implications and the Gateway Bridge

1. **"I am not in my body" is real — and producible.** The intensity of an experience gives no information about its accuracy. This distinction is **critical** in both the clinic (dissociation) and spiritual practice.
2. **Threshold management.** Weakening vestibular and tactile references (darkness, stillness, thermal constancy, rhythmic sound) facilitates self-location drift; this is the laboratory counterpart of the traditional "dark room / movement deprivation" practice.
3. **Preserving channel separation.** "Self-location can drift" neither proves nor refutes the claim that "consciousness can leave the body". The honest position: the mechanism is known, the evidence claim is **open.**
4. **Clinical caution.** Out-of-body experiences can be associated with migraine, temporal-lobe epilepsy, vestibular disorders, dissociative disorders and some drug/medication effects. New or frequently recurring cases require neurological assessment.
5. **Research note:** this file connects to UA-15 (physiology of death), UA-05 (filter curve), UA-31 (liminality) and UA-40 (the measurement problem); the shared question: **how is the observer's place in space measured?**

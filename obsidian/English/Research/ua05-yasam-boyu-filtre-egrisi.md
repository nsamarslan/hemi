# UA-05 — The Lifespan Filter Curve: Infantile Amnesia, Childhood Memories and Terminal Lucidity

This dossier examines the same strange phenomenon appearing at three separate ends of a human life — birth, the seven-year threshold, and the deathbed: when the narrative self has not yet been built, or is dissolving, memory and awareness behave unusually. We end up proposing a **U-shaped filter curve** across the lifespan: the narrative filter (DMN) is not fully closed at birth but closes gradually; it locks around age seven; it loosens again at the death threshold. This gives a single frame for two questions: "why can I not remember my infancy?" and "why did my dying relative become lucid for a moment?"

## 1. Intersection Map: Why Do Developmental Neuroscience, Hospice Records and the History of Religions All Mark the Same Age Seven?

Five lines run in parallel here:

1. **Developmental electrophysiology** — the frequency architecture of infant and child EEG.
2. **Network maturation studies** — the default mode network (DMN) and the myelination timetable.
3. **Memory development** — infantile amnesia and measurement of the earliest-memory age.
4. **Hospice/aging records** — terminal and paradoxical lucidity.
5. **History of religions and developmental psychology** — the "age of reason" marked independently by many cultures.

They do not merge, because merging them yields this sentence: **the narrative self is not pre-installed hardware but a module with a timetable — and while that module is open or closing, experience is recorded in another format.** This sentence breaks both the view that "babies are like us but forget" and the metaphor that "aging memory = diminishing disk".

## 2. Anomaly Inventory (with citations)

### 2.1 Earliest-memory age: about three and a half years

The average earliest memory reported by adults sits around **3.5 years** in many independent samples (Hayne, 2000, *Developmental Review* 20:21–34; Peterson, Warren & Short, 2011, *Child Development* 82:1152–1167). The spread is wide (2–8 years) and reports before that age are rare. The anomaly: infants **do learn** — language, faces, routes, fears — but that learning does not turn into tellable memory.

Freud (1905) named this gap "infantile amnesia" and explained it by repression. Modern data do not support repression: the amnesia is not limited to traumatic content; it covers the **entire** early period.

### 2.2 Infant EEG: slow waves even while awake

The waking EEG of a newborn differs fundamentally from an adult's: power concentrates in the **delta and theta** bands; the posterior alpha rhythm only emerges over the first months and drifts toward adult frequency over years (Marshall, Bar-Haim & Fox, 2002, *Developmental Neuropsychology* 22:423–440; Anderson & Perone, 2018, *Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience* 33:1–11). The human infant, even during "awake" hours, lives in the band an adult would call **deep relaxation/meditation**.

### 2.3 Sleep architecture: the REM percentage falls across the lifespan

Roffwarg, Muzio and Dement (1966, *Science* 152:604–619) showed that the proportion of REM sleep declines systematically across life: about half of total sleep in newborns, about a fifth in adults. The infant is slow-wave dominant while awake and REM-heavy while asleep — the period in which the narrative filter is presumably weakest is also the most "dream-like" period.

### 2.4 The default network matures late

Fair et al. (2008, *PNAS* 105:4028–4032) and later network-maturation work showed DMN functional connectivity **increasing through childhood**; the myelination timetable (Yakovlev & Lecours, 1967) shows prefrontal and parietal association areas among the last to mature. The carrier of the narrative self is not ready at birth; it is **built**.

### 2.5 The five-to-seven shift and the "age of reason"

Developmental psychology describes the 5–7 range as a **qualitative jump**: executive function, theory of mind, source memory and the autobiographical frame maturing together (Sameroff & Haith, 1996, *The Five to Seven Year Shift*). This jump independently coincides with the following cultural thresholds:

| Tradition | Marked age | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Christianity | 7 | "Age of reason" — first confession/communion |
| Judaism (Pirkei Avot 5:21) | 5–7 | Start of education; responsibility at 13 |
| Islam | 7 | Start of prayer instruction |
| Hinduism | 7–8 | Upanayana (sacred thread) |
| Ancient Greece (Aristotle, *Politics*) | 7 | The age at which reason separates |

Five independent traditions marked the same age as a threshold of **responsibility and memory**. This is not a religious coincidence; it is the cultural reading of a developmental timetable.

### 2.6 The age distribution of childhood memories: speaking and silencing

In the long case series of the University of Virginia's Division of Perceptual Studies (DOPS), children reporting memories of previous lives typically **begin between ages 2 and 4** and **fall silent around 6–7** (Stevenson, 2001, *Children Who Remember Previous Lives*; Tucker, 2005, *Life Before Life*; Haraldsson, 2003, *Psychology and Psychotherapy* 76:1–22).

The honesty note matters: these series are **not controlled**, carry selection bias, and their content claims (previous lives) are not this dossier's subject. But the **age distribution** is independently striking: onset coincides with the building of the linguistic self; silence begins when the narrative filter locks.

### 2.7 Terminal lucidity: the window where the filter loosens

Nahm and Greyson (2009, *Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease* 197:942–944) assembled documented cases of **terminal lucidity** in patients with chronic schizophrenia and dementia: as clouding of consciousness deepened, shortly before death, patients briefly "returned to themselves". Mashour et al. (2019, *Alzheimer's & Dementia* 15:1107–1114) conceptualized this as "paradoxical lucidity" and proposed a neurobiological research program.

These records are retrospective and carry selection bias, but the pattern is consistent: **as externally directed information processing declines, the unity of the self briefly blazes.**

### 2.8 The childhood NDE pattern

Children's near-death experiences carry features strikingly similar to adults' (tunnel, light, a "holistic knowing" rather than a life review). This suggests the experience is not cultural learning but the **fixed output of a particular physiological state** — an unsurprising conclusion for this dossier: the same state produces the same basic pattern.

## 3. Three-Level Excavation

### Level 1 — Observation
- The earliest adult memory averages ~3.5 years; infants learn but cannot narrate.
- Infants are delta-theta dominant when awake; the REM proportion falls across life.
- The DMN and myelination mature late; a qualitative jump occurs at 5–7.
- Five independent traditions mark age ~7 as a threshold.
- In DOPS cases, narration begins at 2–4 and fades at 6–7.
- At the death threshold (terminal/paradoxical lucidity) the self briefly reunifies.

### Level 2 — Pattern
A single curve emerges: **the strength of the narrative filter F(t) is not an inverted U but a U:**
- **0–1 years:** F ≈ near zero (no narrator yet; experience in a different format).
- **1–7 years:** F rises gradually (language, self-recognition, autobiographical indexing).
- **7–50+ years:** F ≈ 1 (adult plateau; the narrative self dominates).
- **End of life:** F loosens again (clouding, terminal-lucidity windows).

And the critical sub-pattern: **memory is encoded in whatever format the filter currently uses.** Experience encoded at F≈0 is not stored in narrative form and is therefore not accessible at F≈1. Infantile amnesia is thus not **erasure** but **format mismatch** — the developmental wing of HZ-16's access-key principle.

### Level 3 — Why is it designed this way?
Because the narrative self is an expensive module: it requires myelination, language, executive function and autobiographical indexing. It **cannot be ready at birth** — a child must experience the world before having the hardware to narrate its own story. The early period is therefore in another coding format: non-narrative, contextual, bodily-sensory.

At the other end of life, the module's **shutdown order breaks**: as physiological collapse proceeds, all resources momentarily turn toward integration. Terminal lucidity is not "memory returning" but **another coding format surfacing as the filter loosens** — which is why patients appear to rebuild not their past but their **presence**.

The cultural consequence: traditions independently found the age at which the F threshold is crossed (≈7) and marked it with **responsibility** — because after that age a person can be considered the author of their own story.

## 4. HIDDEN PATTERN CANDIDATE: HZ-05 — The U-Shaped Filter Curve

> **Candidate Law (HZ-05).** The strength of the narrative-self filter F(t) follows a U across life: low at birth, full around age seven, constant through adulthood, low again at life's end. The encoding format of experience depends on F(t); early-period memories are therefore inaccessible to adult narrative because they were never stored in narrative form.

Simplified expression:

**Access(adult narrative) ≈ Content × δ(encoding_format, retrieval_format)** &nbsp;&nbsp; where δ is the format match.

**Sub-law (HZ-05a — amnesia as format mismatch).** Infantile amnesia is not content loss but a coding-access format difference. Two predictions follow: (i) early content should be more accessible through **non-narrative** routes (odour, music, touch, bodily state); (ii) the individual age at which F locks should shift the earliest-memory age.

**Discriminating test (HZ-05/1 — the locking timetable).** A longitudinal study: DMN functional connectivity and myelination proxies (sleep spindle density, diffusion measures) measured yearly in children; the same children report their earliest memories at 18–25. Prediction: children whose DMN locks **earlier** have an **older** (later) earliest-memory age — the filter cuts early content from narrative.

**Second test (HZ-05/2 — recovery by format matching).** In dementia and normal aging samples, early childhood (0–7) content is probed with three cue types: verbal question, odour/music cue, bodily-state cue. Prediction: early content is significantly more accessible in the **odour/music** arm (consistent with HZ-16's key principle).

**Third test (HZ-05/3 — the terminal window).** In terminal/paradoxical lucidity cases, simultaneous recording where possible (EEG; witness data complementary). Prediction: the lucid window coincides with **increased global slow-wave/gamma coupling** and reduced within-DMN connectivity (overlapping UA-15's gamma-surge finding). If the prediction partly fails, the "death side" of the curve weakens.

## 5. Evidence Thermometer: 🟢 (birth side) / 🟡 (seven-year threshold and terminal side)

**Why 🟢 (birth side)?** Infant EEG architecture, the decline of REM proportion with age, the late maturation of the DMN and the ~3.5-year earliest-memory age have been measured repeatedly in independent laboratories. This direction is solid.

**Why 🟡 (threshold and end)?** The qualitative-jump reading of the 5–7 shift, and the claim that cultural age markers are its reading, are **theoretical framing**, not measurement. Terminal lucidity is retrospective and open to selection bias (Nahm & Greyson 2009 is a case collection; Mashour 2019 is a research call).

> **If refuted, what survives?** Even if the death side of the U collapses, the developmental side stands: infantile amnesia remains a fact, and the "format mismatch" account is independently supported by the superiority of odour/music cues and by state-dependency findings. In no scenario does the dossier empty out; only the right arm of the curve falls, the left remains.

## 6. Practical Implications and the Gateway Bridge

1. **The early period is a high-access window.** Ages 0–7 are the period before the narrative filter fully locks; early experiences (fear, trust, safety) are therefore coded as **bodily-emotional** templates that operate across a lifetime. For parenting, this is a consequence beyond measure.
2. **"I cannot remember, yet it affects me."** This sentence is no longer a mystery but a mechanism: content may never have entered narrative, yet it operates through body and behavior.
3. **Access routes change in old age.** As narrative probing (questions, dates) weakens, sensory probing (music, odour, touch, rhythm) stays comparatively strong. Care practice should therefore ask "through which channel does this person remember?" rather than declare "they do not remember".
4. **Terminal lucidity carries a moral consequence.** A dying patient's moment of clarity should be read as "a window opened", not "they are recovering"; relatives using that moment for witnessing and farewell produces the benefit the records repeatedly show. This does not change treatment decisions but deeply changes communication practice.
5. **Caution.** This dossier passes no verdict on post-mortem claims and does not validate childhood reports; validation belongs to controlled studies. As a health caution: in dementia and terminal care, organic causes of behavioral change (infection, medication, metabolic disturbance) must always be excluded first — a lucid window may also signal a treatable condition.
6. **Research note:** this file can be read as the **ontogenetic fractal** of Φ-1 (the planetary macro-DMN): within a personal history, ages 0–7 resemble humanity's 30,000 BCE–1600 CE — a low-filter period without instruments (high AOL noise). The analogy is a proposal only; its test will be built with UA-38 and UA-10.

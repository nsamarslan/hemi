# UA-35 — The Elasticity of Time Perception: How the Brain Produces Time and Why It Stretches

This dossier examines why the human brain carries no single "internal clock" and why subjective time is therefore warped by state, attention and emotion. The stretching of time under stress and the timing errors produced by dopamine systems emerge as two faces of the same architecture. We end with a candidate law defining time as a **fabricated patch** rather than something perceived.

## 1. Intersection Map: Neuropharmacology, Experimental Psychology and Physics Ask the Same Question

Four lines intersect here:

1. **Timing neuroscience** — the neural correlates of sub-second and supra-second intervals.
2. **Neuropharmacology** — the effect of dopamine systems on timing.
3. **Experimental psychology** — time warping under fear, attention and load.
4. **Fundamental physics and information theory** — the measurement of time and the uncertainty principle (Δt·Δω).

Why do they not merge? Because merging them produces this sentence: **subjective time is not a counter counting events but a derivative of how much detail was written to memory.** This breaks the assumption that time maps one-to-one onto a world clock — and builds an unexpected kinship with the uncertainty principle.

## 2. Anomaly Inventory (with citations)

### 2.1 Expectancy theory and the local counter

Gibbon (1977, *Psychological Review* 84:279–302), in "scalar expectancy theory", proposed that time is estimated by an **internal counter** (pacemaker plus accumulator). The classical model assumes time flows at one rate and carries only calibration error. It explains much data but cannot alone answer "why does time stretch under fear?"

### 2.2 The cortico-striatal oscillator model: not a clock but a coincidence detector

Matell and Meck (2004, *Cognitive Brain Research* 21:139–170), in the striatal beat-frequency model, describe timing as **coincidence detection of cortical oscillator arrays** in the striatum. The brain is not a stopwatch but a detector reading **which oscillator pattern coincides when**. This is exactly where HP-1 (the multi-scale rhythm cascade) connects to timing: time is **read** from the phase relationships of oscillations.

### 2.3 Dopamine: the gain of time

- Meck (1996, *Journal of Neuroscience* 16:6166–6176) showed dopamine antagonists systematically shifting interval estimates.
- Coull, Cheng and Meck (2011, *Neuropsychopharmacology* 36:3–25) showed dopaminergic modulation acting on **perceptual timing** distinctly from motor timing.
- Methylphenidate studies (Soares et al., 2016) further blurred the boundary between attention and timing.

The critical inference: timing is not a rigid clock error but a **gain-adjusted** estimate — exactly what MP-3 predicts: change the critical parameter and the "measured quantity" shifts.

### 2.4 Fear and time: a two-layer warping

The findings here look contradictory at first glance:

- Noyes and Kletti (1977) compiled reports of **time slowing** in life-threatening situations.
- Stetson, Fiesta and Eagleman (2007, *PLoS ONE* 2:e1295) showed in a free-fall experiment that participants reported time slowing but **online temporal resolution did not increase**: the brain was not sampling faster in real time. Memory density increased instead.
- Campbell and Bryant (2007, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 14:822–828) confirmed that **retrospective** time estimates inflate after life-threatening events.

Read together: **the sense that "time stretched" is not an online slowdown but a retrospective memory-density calculation.**

### 2.5 Prospective vs. retrospective: two different times

Zakay and Block (2004; Block, Hancock & Zakay, 2010, *Acta Psychologica* 134:330–343) — a meta-analysis — established a dual distinction: **prospective** timing (you are counting now) relates **inversely** to attention: when attention is diverted, duration feels "short". **Retrospective** timing (you recall later) relates **directly** to memory density: when the event was rich, duration feels "long". One question, two answers: "how long did it take?" starts a different calculation **depending on when it is asked**.

### 2.6 The oddball effect: time leaping at unexpected moments

In oddball paradigms an unexpected, rare stimulus subjectively appears "longer" (Tse et al., 2004, *Perception & Psychophysics* 66:1171–1189; Dürschmid et al., 2016, *PNAS* 113:4104–4109 on prediction error and timing). Time is modulated not only by attention but by **prediction error**: the brain allocates extra resources to the unexpected event, and that extra processing appears as "added" duration.

### 2.7 Long-term practice: meditation and subjective time

Wittmann's work (e.g. Wittmann & Schmidt, 2014, *Frontiers in Psychology* 5:673) shows systematic differences in duration estimates among regular meditators: estimates are less inflated and the "present moment" experience widens. This is consistent with the prospective model: withdraw the attention that feeds the internal counter and duration does not "grow".

### 2.8 A physical match: time as an uncertainty relation

MP-2 proposed the time-frequency conjugate uncertainty (Δt·Δω ≥ 1/2). UA-35 measures the **cognitive** reflection of that principle: a precise estimate of a "moment" requires processing a wide frequency band; a system operating in a narrow band cannot sharpen the boundary of the "moment". The brain's temporal resolution is therefore in a **trade-off** with the bandwidth it processes — a physical ground for why subjective time depends on perceptual state.

## 3. Three-Level Excavation

### Level 1 — Observation
- Subjective time is systematically warped by state, attention and emotion.
- In fear, time "stretches" but online resolution does not rise; memory density does.
- Prospective and retrospective timing run in opposite directions.
- Dopamine systems shift the gain of timing.
- Unexpected events appear subjectively longer.
- Duration overestimation falls in meditators.

### Level 2 — Pattern
A three-component pattern:

**(A) Not a counter, a reader.** Time is not a stopwatch output but the reading of phase relations among many oscillators (Matell & Meck 2004). Gain (dopamine, attention) therefore directly changes the read value.

**(B) Two different computations.** "How long is this taking?" and "how long did it take?" are separate operations: the first is **inversely** related to attention, the second **directly** related to memory density.

**(C) Warping = an uncertainty trade-off.** As temporal resolution rises, processed bandwidth narrows and vice versa; the "elasticity" of subjective time is the experiential face of this trade-off.

### Level 3 — Why is it designed this way?
Because an absolute clock would be **useless** to the organism. The survival questions are not "what time is it?" but "how much time do I have for this situation?" and "how long did that event last, and what should I learn from it?" The second question depends on memory richness: a rich memory encodes as longer — because **the amount of encoded material** is a proxy for duration.

The brain therefore does not measure time; it **derives time from event density.** This is not a defect but an economical engineering solution: event density is already being recorded (entropy increase in information-theoretic terms), and time arrives as a **free by-product** of that record. Time stretching in fear is not a malfunction but the inevitable side effect of this architecture.

## 4. HIDDEN PATTERN CANDIDATE: HZ-35 — The Internal Clock Patch

> **Candidate Law (HZ-35).** The brain does not measure time; it **estimates** it from the phase readings of many oscillators and from event density. Subjective duration splits into two computations: prospective (inverse to attention) and retrospective (direct with memory density). Both are gain-adjusted; dopamine and prediction error shift that gain.

Simplified expression:

**T_subj ≈ (N_events / ρ_memory) × W_attention × K_dopamine** &nbsp;&nbsp; and &nbsp; **Δt · Δf_osc ≈ constant** (a cognitive uncertainty trade-off)

**Three predictions:**
1. **Fear = density, not speed:** during an intense event, online temporal resolution should not rise, while **memory density** and retrospective duration should (Stetson 2007).
2. **Dopamine = gain, not clock:** dopaminergic drugs should shift timing error in a **directional, dose-dependent** way, while timing variance should track bandwidth changes rather than clock changes.
3. **Meditation = withdrawal from the counter:** regular practice should reduce prospective duration overestimation and leave retrospective density estimates unchanged.

**Discriminating test (HZ-35/1).** A three-arm experiment: (A) an attention-demanding task, (B) safe thrill (e.g. high-speed VR scenario), (C) regular meditation. Each arm measures both prospective and retrospective duration and counts event density (number of details encoded). Prediction: in arm B, prospective duration **does not change**, retrospective duration and detail count **rise**; in arm C, prospective overestimation **falls**.

**Second test (HZ-35/2 — the uncertainty trade-off).** Participants receive stimuli of different bandwidths (e.g. broadband noise vs. narrowband tone) while timing resolution is measured. Prediction: resolution **falls** in broadband (Δt grows) and **rises** in narrowband; the relationship should correlate with the individual α/θ peak frequency (Klimesch 2018, IAF).

**Third test (HZ-35/3 — oddball and entropy).** Correlate duration overestimation with EEG entropy for unexpected stimuli. Prediction: overestimation relates **directly** to entropy increase; when attention is entered as a control variable, the effect persists (prediction error, not attention, is decisive).

## 5. Evidence Thermometer: 🟢 (mechanism and warping patterns demonstrated; a single unified model is contested)

**Why 🟢?** Dopamine shifting timing, oscillator-based models, the prospective/retrospective distinction and the memory-density origin of the fear effect were shown in independent studies; designs such as Stetson 2007 directly tested the "slowing" claim.

**Why not a perfect score?** (1) There is no single neural "time circuit"; distributed models compete. (2) Sub-second and supra-second timing rely on different mechanisms. (3) The uncertainty trade-off (HZ-35's sub-claim) has not yet been tested directly at the cognitive level.

> **If refuted, what survives?** Even if the "reader, not counter" claim collapses, the prospective/retrospective distinction and the memory-density relation remain — independently replicated findings. The dossier keeps its core architecture even if it loses its weakest link (the physical trade-off).

## 6. Practical Implications and the Gateway Bridge

1. **Time does not lie; it estimates.** The sense that "duration stretched" is a calculation, not a defect. This is why traumatic memories feel vast and ordinary days feel short: memory density converts directly into duration.
2. **Why does time vanish in deep focus?** Because the prospective counter is not fed; the "present moment" widens. Distorted time estimates during Gateway sessions are a natural consequence of this mechanism — neither pathology nor "proof", simply architecture.
3. **Meditation is not time training, but it trains time.** Regular practice changes the weight of attention in time production; this is a measurable advantage in anxiety management (the perception of time pressure falls).
4. **Caution.** "Time slippage" complaints after intense stress and trauma can be symptoms of dissociative and anxiety disorders; no self-administered practice substitutes for clinical assessment.
5. **Research note:** this file forms a "time processing" family with UA-36 (interoception), UA-08 (sleep), UA-29 (retrocausality) and UA-32 (flow).

# UA-34 — Lunar Cycle and Biological Tides: How Firm Is the Evidence?

**Yes in the sea; uncertain and probably small in humans.** For the marine annelid *Platynereis dumerilii*, an endogenous monthly reproductive clock can be set by dim light and continues under free-running conditions. In humans, the finding of about **20 minutes** less sleep around a full moon in a 33-person laboratory analysis has not been consistently reproduced by larger independent studies. Longitudinal wrist records from three Toba/Qom communities and Seattle students, however, report later and shorter sleep **3–5 nights before** a full moon. In January 2026 the same research line released a **non-peer-reviewed preprint** proposing that **gravitational variation** near both full and new moon correlates with sleep in humans and titi monkeys. This is not a conclusion that “the moon controls the brain”; it is a new competing hypothesis clarifying whether light or tides should be measured.

## 1. Intersection Map: Chronobiology × Field Ethnography × Marine Ecology × Folklore

The moon has a **29.53-day synodic phase** (illumination pattern) and roughly a **14.77-day spring–neap / gravitational-variation** rhythm. Full and new moon are both strong alignments of the Sun–Earth–Moon system for tides; in illumination they are opposites. The **early-evening moonlight before full moon** might shift bedtimes where electric light is scarce; even this would not require an **internal monthly clock**. For creatures living in the intertidal zone, water depth and night light are much more direct ecological cues. “Humans are 70% water, so they must have ocean-like tides” is a false analogy: what drives ocean tides is not water percentage but **gravity gradients across large distances**, geometry and resonance.

The evidence questions must be separated: *is there an endogenous oscillator in a marine animal?* Yes, in some species. *Is human sleep associated with lunar phase?* Contradictory findings by design and cohort. *What causes any association?* Light, social behavior, temperature, calendar sampling, clock geometry and other environmental variables compete. *Does a lunar energy field create consciousness?* There is no measurement bridging that final leap.

## 2. Anomaly Inventory: Small but Genuine Challenges

### 2.1 The laboratory finding and the file drawer

Cajochen et al. (2013, *Current Biology* 23, 1485–1488; DOI 10.1016/j.cub.2013.06.029) retrospectively aligned previously collected dark-laboratory sleep from **33** volunteers with lunar phase. Close to full moon, deep-sleep delta activity was approximately **30% lower**, sleep onset **five minutes longer**, total sleep **20 minutes shorter**, and melatonin and subjective quality reduced. The pattern under conditions where moonlight was unseen is interesting; but phase analysis happened **after data collection** and 33 people are few for multiple sleep outcomes.

Cordi et al. (2014, *Current Biology*, R549–R550) did not reproduce the result across three larger sleep datasets and noted the invisibility of unpublished null results (*file drawer*). Smith et al. (2014) found some sleep changes in 47 young volunteers but **the direction of REM latency disagreed with Cajochen**; age and sex imbalance across phase groups may matter. Haba-Rubio et al. (2015, *Sleep Medicine* 16, 1321–1326) found no substantial association in **2,125 adults** with home polysomnography. Null findings do not prove an effect is exactly zero in every context; they do narrow claims of a universal, large full-moon effect.

### 2.2 Large sample, small difference

Chaput et al. (2016) studied about **33,710 accelerometer nights from 5,812 children in 12 countries**: sleep around full moon was about **five minutes** shorter than near new moon, with no meaningful activity pattern. Statistical detection is not the same as a major practical effect: five minutes, about one percent of sleep, is easily swamped by time zone, weekday/holiday, screens and exercise. This difference does not create a clinical “full-moon insomnia” diagnosis.

### 2.3 The field study: ask about evenings before full moon, not full moon itself

Casiraghi et al. (2021, *Science Advances* 7, eabe0465; DOI 10.1126/sciadv.abe0465) examined repeated wrist-measured nights in **three Toba/Qom communities with different electricity access** in Argentina and a Seattle student cohort. **Three to five days before** full moon, when moonlight arrives early in the night, sleep starts later and lasts less time. Across communities, phase-dependent differences could reach **46–58 minutes**; this is **not an average to be applied to every human**. A stronger pattern with little access to artificial light is plausible but not conclusive support for a **moonlight mediator**. Wrist actigraphy infers sleep from motion; repeated observations on individuals are a strength, differences between cohorts and lack of direct individual light exposure are limitations.

**Current inflection (2026 preprint):** Rodríguez Ferrante, Casiraghi et al., *Lunar gravity predicts sleep timing* (bioRxiv DOI 10.64898/2026.01.27.701907), propose correlations between gravitational-variation peaks shared by new and full moon and sleep in Seattle and Toba/Qom, also reporting captive titi monkeys with attenuated natural light. This **has not passed peer review** and partly reinterprets earlier longitudinal records from the same research network; it is not a fully independent replication. Its abstract notes that **other covarying geophysical factors are possible**. Prespecified lags, scanning multiple periods, light/culture/weekly calendar, data reuse and environmental specificity require further testing. **A tidal association may exist; direct lunar gravitational causation of human sleep is not established.**

### 2.4 Marine comparison: a genuine second clock

Zantke et al. (2013, *Cell Reports*) separated **daily** and **monthly** clocks in *Platynereis dumerilii*. Monthly reproductive synchrony was **entrained by dim nocturnal light**, then persisted as an approximately **30-day endogenous rhythm** under certain constant conditions; disruption of circadian gene oscillations did not abolish the lunar rhythm. Later molecular work from the Tessmar-Raible line investigated protein-level signatures of its circalunar clock. This is strong evidence for **marine monthly timing**; transferring a light-entrained reproductive oscillator to gravitational control of human sleep is a mistaken shift of scale and mechanism.

### 2.5 What can each study actually conclude?

## 3. Three-Level Excavation

**Level 1 — Observation:** strong endogenous monthly timing exists in a marine species. In humans, estimates of lunar sleep duration/timing range across study designs from null to ~5 minutes, ~20 minutes and larger cohort-specific swings. Averaging these into one number is unsound: outcomes, samples and instruments differ.

**Level 2 — Hidden pattern:** the lunar cycle is not *one clock* but a bundle of phase-related yet distinct **light × gravity × social calendar** signals. “Everyone loses sleep in the same way at full moon” is weak because it fails to separate evenings before full moon for light from both full and new moon for gravity. If a new-moon peak persists when light is controlled, gravity gains plausibility — though another environmental synchronizer remains possible.

**Level 3 — Historical pattern:** tidal observations in folk knowledge → 33 laboratory sleepers in darkness → the file drawer and large nulls → real-world actigraphy → a 2026 gravimetry preprint. Science here is not a straight line but a set of **mechanisms narrowing one another**. The myth “the moon governs us all” flattens both strong marine biology and weak human generalization equally.

## 4. HIDDEN PATTERN CANDIDATE: HZ-34 — Circadian–Lunar Modulation

> **Candidate law:** If lunar cues affect human sleep, the effect should appear not as a direct “internal ocean tide,” but as **modulation of the daily clock by environmental input**; separate explanatory contributions of light and gravity must be declared ahead of analysis.

`ΔSleep_i,t ≈ β_L · L_evening,t + β_G · G_gravity,t + β_S · S_calendar,i,t + u_i + ε_i,t`. `L` is measured individual evening light (not just astronomical phase), `G` a site-specific Moon+Sun gravimetric/tidal proxy, `S` work/school/weekend and cultural conditions; `u_i` the person effect. The β coefficients are **hypotheses tested against zero**, not a discovered physical law. Because phases are correlated, a single lunar cycle cannot distinguish the coefficients.

**Discriminating test /1:** follow participants over **at least six lunar cycles** at multiple latitudes and levels of artificial lighting; wrist actigraphy plus a subsample with polysomnography, melatonin and sleep diaries, personal evening-light sensors, cloud cover, moon visibility near bedtime, weekly social schedule and weather. Analysis locked in advance: (a) bright evenings before full moon, (b) two gravitational peaks including new moon, (c) randomly phase-shifted sham moon cycles. Preregister sleep onset within individuals as primary outcome; total duration is secondary. Success requires the **same sign and conditional effect in an external cohort**, while sham phases stay null. Adjust for searches across multiple time lags.

**Discriminating test /2:** a light-mediator prediction: β_L should diminish when outdoor light is measured and blocked; if only β_G survives, light is not the whole explanation. A gravimetric comparison must control other covarying geophysical factors, temperature, geomagnetism and circadian phase; the preprint alone cannot prove the moon *directly* pulls on the body to change sleep. The marine oscillator can serve as a positive control for /3; until an internally free-running rhythm persists in a human sleep lab for **multiple cycles**, do not claim a “human monthly clock.”

## 5. Evidence Thermometer and Falsification Box

**🟢** A marine monthly oscillator; light/environmental effects on human sleep timing are ordinary physiology. **🟡** The human sleep–moon association is mixed by cohort and endpoint but visible in some repeated measures; size and generalizability are unsettled. **🟠** An independent human monthly clock, a gravity-specific mechanism beyond light and a qualitative “tide of consciousness” have not been established; the 2026 work is a **preprint**. **🔴** Absolute claims that a full moon determines everyone's behavior, psychosis, crime or metaphysical experience lack support.

> **If falsified, what remains?** If both β_L and β_G are zero over several cycles in humans with measured light, **the marine worm's genuine monthly clock, the human 24-hour circadian system and the physical reality of moonlight** remain; the sleep claim narrows. If only light persists, environmental scheduling remains without a gravity or consciousness claim. If a gravity link independent of light replicates, a new mechanism investigation begins — not a proclamation of direct gravitational causation or paranormal effects.

---

## 6. Practical Implications and the Gateway Bridge

Sleep decisions should rest on **light exposure, regular timing, safety and a personal sleep log**, not a phase calendar; sleep disorders merit professional assessment. The quality of Gateway sessions may change with morning/evening, sleep debt and expectation. Lunar phase can be an *observational covariate* in a journal, but “consciousness rises at full moon” cannot be a product promise. UA-33's external calibration and HZ-07's sidereal-time control yield the same rule for cosmic clocks: **measure local exposure and sham phase**.

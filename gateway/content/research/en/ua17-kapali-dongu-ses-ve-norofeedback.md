# UA-17 — Closed-Loop Sound and Neurofeedback: Steering the Brain's Phase from Outside

This dossier examines how sleep and attention architecture can be steered by externally delivered periodic sound. It places open-loop fixed-difference-frequency approaches (Hemi-Sync and its relatives) next to the laboratory-evidenced technique of closed-loop, phase-locked stimulation, and shows why only the latter counts as writing phase in the technical sense. It closes with a candidate law: when a neural oscillator does not have to generate its own rhythm, an externally supplied regular drive can carry cognitive content.

## 1. Intersection Map: Why Five Literatures Are Running the Same Experiment

Five research lines operate here in ignorance of one another, yet all ask the same question:

1. **Sleep neuroscience** — if you place sound at the phase of the slow oscillation (0.5–1 Hz), does memory consolidate better?
2. **Clinical neurofeedback** — can you teach a person a frequency band by showing or sounding their own EEG (a line running since Sterman's cat work on SMR)?
3. **Neuromodulation research** — does 40 Hz sound and light clear amyloid plaques in mice, and improve cognition in humans (the GENUS line)?
4. **Neuromusicology** — how does periodic rhythm lock auditory and motor cortex, and how does the EEG "sit" on a drum pattern (measured since Neher in 1961)?
5. **Commercial audio technology** — binaural beats, Hemi-Sync, isochronic tones: play a fixed difference and assume the brain follows.

Why did these five never merge? Because merging them produces an uncomfortable sentence: **the brain's rhythm, at least in some states, is not an internal clock but an environmental grip.** That sentence threatens three fields at once — representational theories that treat consciousness as internal computation, a naive behaviorism that says "it is only a stimulus", and the fixed-frequency assumption of the commercial audio industry.

This dossier makes that sentence testable.

## 2. Anomaly Inventory (with citations)

### 2.1 Closed-loop auditory stimulation: the strongest evidence line

Ngo, Martinetz, Born and Mölle (2013, *Neuron* 78:545–553) monitored sleeping participants in real time and placed a 50 ms burst of pink noise at the **rising peak of the slow oscillation**. The result: slow-oscillation power increased, sleep spindles increased, and next-day word-pair recall improved significantly. The critical detail: the sound was **triggered by the brain's own phase**. The same sound at the same intensity, delivered at random times, loses the effect.

Papalambros et al. (2017, *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 11:109) carried the protocol into older adults and reported results in the same direction. The shared conclusion of this line: **the effect does not come from the sound itself but from the locking of sound to brain phase.** What is being measured is not sound therapy; it is a timing technology.

### 2.2 40 Hz gamma entrainment: strong animal data, contested human generalization

Iaccarino et al. (2016, *Nature* 540:230–235) delivered 40 Hz sound and light to mice and observed reduced amyloid load and altered microglial activity. Adaikkan et al. (2019, *Neuron* 102:929–943) reported that entrainment spread to higher-order cortex and produced neuroprotective signatures; Martorell et al. (2019, *Cell* 177:256–271) reported that multisensory 40 Hz stimulation improved cognition. This is the field's most provocative finding: **an external rhythm can change tissue pathology through the immune clearance system.**

The human side demands caution. The methodological review by Thut, Schyns and Gross (2011, *Frontiers in Psychology* 2:170) shows that rhythmic stimulation does not easily produce lasting, generalized entrainment in human cortex; the 40 Hz auditory effect often stays near auditory cortex with small cognitive generalization. "Proven in mice" is, for humans, an unfinished sentence.

### 2.3 Neurofeedback: clinically useful, but why?

Sterman and Friar (1972, *EEG and Clinical Neurophysiology* 33:89–95) reduced seizure frequency in an epilepsy patient by rewarding the SMR band (12–15 Hz). Peniston and Kulkosky (1989, *Alcoholism: Clinical and Experimental Research* 13:271–279; 1991, *Medical Psychotherapy* 4:47–60) reported markedly reduced relapse and PTSD symptoms using an alpha-theta protocol.

The crack inside the field is this: Arns et al. (2009, *Clinical EEG and Neuroscience* 40:180–189) found moderate effects in ADHD, while Micoulaud-Franchi et al. (2014, *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 8:906) showed the effect shrinking under **blinded assessment**. The debate is therefore not "does it work?" but "if it works, is it EEG learning, or the attention-rest-expectation triad?"

### 2.4 Rhythmic driving: the oldest measurement

Neher (1961–62) showed that drum-like stimulation at 4–7 beats per second produces **driving** in the human occipital EEG — the first laboratory proof that brain rhythm settles onto an external rhythm. Today neuromusicology describes beat perception as a predictive, bidirectional mechanism in auditory and motor cortex (Large, Herrera & Velasco, 2015, *Frontiers in Systems Neuroscience* 9:153).

### 2.5 The commercial line: binaural beats

The meta-analysis by Garcia-Argibay, Santed and Reales (2019, *Psychological Research* 83:357–372) shows that binaural beats have **small but measurable** effects on anxiety, memory and pain. This places the commercial claims of Hemi-Sync-style products neither in the "nothing at all" camp nor in the "everything" camp. Crucially, the fixed difference frequency is **open loop**: the device does not know where the brain is.

### 2.6 A fourth level: individual oscillation, individual identity

Klimesch (2018, *European Journal of Neuroscience* 48:2431–2453) demonstrated that the frequency architecture of human bands (delta-theta-alpha-beta-gamma) is not fixed across individuals, that the individual alpha peak frequency (IAF) behaves like a fingerprint, and that all band boundaries shift with it. For every open-loop technology emitting a fixed difference frequency, this single finding predicts a measurable error term: **a default 10 Hz alpha misses the target in a person whose peak is 8.3 Hz.**

## 3. Three-Level Excavation

### Level 1 — Observation (what happens?)

- An external periodic stimulus produces amplitude increase at the corresponding frequency (driving).
- When the drive carries **phase** information (locked to brain phase), behavior changes; when it does not, behavior does not change.
- In some cases the effect reaches cell biology (microglia, plaque clearance).
- The effect depends on the individual's own peak frequency.

### Level 2 — Pattern (what shape is this?)

These four observations collapse into one pattern: **an external rhythm can substitute for the brain's internal rhythm — but only under two conditions: (i) the frequency is close enough to the individual's peak frequency, and (ii) delivery is locked to the brain's own phase gate.**

This is a classical case of *injection locking*, a standard result of nonlinear oscillator physics: if the frequency difference (Δω) between two oscillators lies inside a capture range, the weaker one is driven; outside it, only a spurious shadow remains (Arnold-tongue-like locking regions). One extra constraint applies: the brain phase is a very noisy oscillator, and noise **widens** the capture range (a stochastic-resonance gain, cf. HP-3). The brain's noisiness is therefore not a defect but the very reason it stays open to external driving.

### Level 3 — Why is it built this way?

A natural objection arises: "Why should the brain be lockable to external rhythm at all? Is having your own clock not an advantage?"

The answer lies in an evolutionary accounting: **the brain never used a single clock.** Heart, respiration, gait, nursing, social tempo — all are different oscillators that must align with external periodic events. No animal experiences independence from the herd's tempo as freedom; moving simultaneously with the group is survival value as real as a chameleon's camouflage. The brain is therefore built to be **open to phase delegation**; an external phase can stand in for the internal one.

Read this not as a weakness but as a **proxy handover**: to save energy, the brain can slow its own generator while someone else produces the rhythm. This is precisely why we are so entrainable in deep sleep.

## 4. HIDDEN PATTERN CANDIDATE: HZ-17 — The Phase Proxy Law

> **Candidate Law (HZ-17).** The cognitive output of a neural oscillator system is determined not by the quality of its internal generator but by who currently **owns the phase**. When an external periodic stimulus enters the capture range and establishes phase lock, the internal generator quiets and cognitive content is carried on the external phase carrier.

Simplified expression:

**SNR_behavior ≈ (K / |Δω|) · L(Φ_brain − Φ_sound)** &nbsp;&nbsp; where L is the probability of phase alignment.

- **K**: coupling coefficient (modality match, sleep depth, autonomic state).
- **Δω**: difference between the individual's peak frequency and the delivered frequency.
- **L(Φ)**: whether the stimulus is placed at the brain's own phase gate (e.g. the rising peak of the slow oscillation).

This law resolves three observations in one equation: (i) why closed loop beats open loop — it maximizes L; (ii) why individual peak frequency determines outcomes — it sets Δω; (iii) why the effect is larger in sleep — K rises and DMN phase noise (HP-2) falls.

**Discriminating test (HZ-17/1).** A four-arm, preregistered study: (A) closed-loop phase-locked sound, (B) yoked control — identical bursts at random phases, (C) continuous tone tuned to the individual's peak frequency, (D) silence. Prediction: A > B ≈ C ≈ D for memory and spindle density. This test directly falsifies or confirms the commercial fixed-frequency assumption — and either outcome advances the literature.

**Second test (HZ-17/2).** Measure IAF in the same person, then deliver driving at the personal peak frequency and at 0.7 Hz off. Prediction: sensitivity falls measurably with Δω, and the shape of the fall resembles an Arnold-tongue capture-band profile.

## 5. Evidence Thermometer: 🟢 (independent laboratory evidence — mechanism demonstrated, human generalization partly open)

**Why 🟢?** The effect of closed-loop auditory stimulation on memory has been replicated by independent laboratories in direction and magnitude; the cellular and tissue-level effects of 40 Hz entrainment were independently shown in mouse models; the clinical effect of neurofeedback (at least in some indications) is supported by meta-analyses. The **existence** of the pattern is not in dispute.

**Why not a perfect score?** Three constraints remain: (1) the cognitive generalization of the 40 Hz effect in humans is contested; (2) which component of neurofeedback produces the effect has not been isolated; (3) open-loop commercial claims (binaural beats) pair small effect sizes with loud marketing.

> **If refuted, what survives?** Nullifying HZ-17 would require closed-loop and yoked control to come out **systematically indistinguishable**; on the current evidence base this is not expected. Should the effect vanish, two things remain: (a) the measurement validity of EEG-based timing technologies, (b) the capacity of rhythmic stimulation to produce subjective relaxation. The dossier would not collapse — it would fall from a "phase writing" claim to a "relaxation tool" claim.

## 6. Practical Implications and the Gateway Bridge

1. **Phase or frequency?** The laboratory evidence says phase is decisive. Every technique emitting a fixed difference frequency (Hemi-Sync included) exploits only the *capture band* portion of the effect and risks missing the individual's peak frequency.
2. **Applied implication:** a personal-difference measurement (resting EEG, or a validated heart-respiration surrogate) should precede any sound protocol. Offering 10 Hz alpha to someone whose peak is 8.3 Hz is like dosing a drug that misses its target.
3. **Gateway bridge:** Monroe's "Focus" levels are a map; Hemi-Sync is an open-loop instrument claiming to travel that map. This dossier says the map may be usable, but the instrument carries a locking-band constraint. The technically strongest version is the one where the feedback loop is closed: brain state is measured, and the stimulus is timed to it.
4. **Caution:** auditory sleep-stimulation protocols applied through commercial devices lacking independent audit carry risks such as sleep fragmentation and headache. Forty-hertz light protocols are contraindicated in people with a history of photosensitive epilepsy.
5. **Research note:** this dossier will connect to later UA-01 (phonetics), UA-18 (rhythm), UA-22 (magnetism) and UA-35 (time perception) files under the same family: an external phase substituting for the internal one.
